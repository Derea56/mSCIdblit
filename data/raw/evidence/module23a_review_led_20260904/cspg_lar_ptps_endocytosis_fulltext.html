Summary

Neural degeneration is a hallmark of spinal cord injury (SCI). Multipotent neural precursor cells (NPCs) have the potential to reconstruct the damaged neuron-glia network due to their tri-lineage capacity to generate neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes. However, astrogenesis is the predominant fate of resident or transplanted NPCs in the SCI milieu adding to the abundant number of resident astrocytes in the lesion. How NPC-derived astrocytes respond to the inflammatory milieu of SCI and the mechanisms by which they contribute to the post-injury recovery processes remain largely unknown. Here, we uncover that activated NPC-derived astrocytes exhibit distinct molecular signature that is immune modulatory and foster neurogenesis, neuronal maturity, and synaptogenesis. Mechanistically, NPC-derived astrocytes perform regenerative matrix remodeling by clearing inhibitory chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs) from the injury milieu through LAR and PTP-σ receptor-mediated endocytosis and the production of ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9, while most resident astrocytes are pro-inflammatory and contribute to the pathologic deposition of CSPGs. These novel findings unravel critical mechanisms of NPC-mediated astrogenesis in SCI repair.

Keywords: spinal cord injury, neural precursor cells, astrocytes, CSPGs remodeling, LAR and PTP-σ receptors, neurogenesis

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) results in significant damage to the neuron-glia network and synaptic loss due to profound inflammation, cell death, axon degeneration, demyelination, and pathological alterations in the extracellular matrix (ECM) (Alizadeh et al., 2019). Restoration of the local circuitry through neurogenesis and gliogenesis is a critical step for spinal cord repair and functional recovery after SCI (Ahuja et al., 2017; Alizadeh et al., 2019; Assinck et al., 2017).

Adult neural precursor cells (NPCs) possess the multi-lineage potential for neurogenesis and gliogenesis (Hosseini et al., 2024). Thus, therapeutic approaches aimed at promoting the response of resident NPCs or transplanting exogenous NPCs have been logically pursued to replenish neurons and oligodendrocytes that are lost after SCI. However, growing evidence has identified a restricted capacity for NPCs to support neurogenesis and oligogenesis in the milieu of SCI, as NPCs give rise to a sizable population of astrocytes (Hosseini et al., 2024). This astrocytic differentiation pattern has been attributed to the extrinsic regulation of NPCs in the injured spinal cord niche that is primarily driven by the pro-inflammatory immune response and the pathologic matrix remodeling associated with the fibrotic scar (Adler et al., 2017; Ceto et al., 2020; Dyck and Karimi-Abdolrezaee, 2015; Hosseini et al., 2022; 2024; Karimi-Abdolrezaee et al., 2006; 2010; Khazaei et al., 2020). We have identified chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs) signaling as a novel inhibitor of neurogenesis and oligogenesis in the injured spinal cord (Dyck et al., 2015, 2019; Hosseini and Karimi-Abdolrezaee, 2025; Hosseini et al., 2022). CSPGs are a major component of the ECM that are markedly upregulated and deposited by activated glia and infiltrating meningeal cells in SCI lesion (Tran et al., 2018). Perturbing CSPGs by chondroitinase ABC or pharmacological blockade of their receptors in vivo significantly promotes the differentiation of transplanted NPCs into neurons and oligodendrocytes and supports the maturity and synaptogenesis of newly generated NPC-derived neurons after SCI (Hosseini et al., 2022; Karimi-Abdolrezaee et al., 2010). Although these and other strategies have improved the multilineage capacity of NPCs, there is still a large number of engrafted NPCs that give rise to new astrocytes adding to the growing population produced through astrogliosis by resident astrocytes in the injured spinal cord (Hosseini et al., 2022, 2024; Karimi-Abdolrezaee et al., 2010; Khazaei et al., 2020). Under injury conditions, astrocytes show heterogeneous response as they can acquire pro-inflammatory, injurious phenotypes or show pro-regeneration characteristics (Hart and Karimi-Abdolrezaee, 2021). Although accumulating evidence suggests that NPC-derived astrocytes are beneficial in SCI (Davies et al., 2006; 2011; Jin et al., 2011; O’Shea et al., 2022), there are critical knowledge gaps on the role and mechanisms of these astrocytes in the injured spinal cord including their inflammatory status, their role in matrix remodeling and scarring, and their influence on the repair processes including NPC properties and fate specification.

Here, we employed extensive in vivo and in vitro investigations using two populations of human primary spinal astrocytes representing resident astrocytes, and astrocytes differentiated from human NPCs. In SCI, we demonstrate that both engrafted and endogenous NPC-derived astrocytes express pro-regenerative cytokines while primary spinal astrocytes show a pro-inflammatory and disease-promoting phenotype characterized by upregulated levels of CSPGs. Transcriptomics and functional studies also revealed a distinct regenerative profile for activated NPC-derived astrocytes associated with neurogenesis, neuronal maturation, and synapse formation while activated primary spinal astrocytes exhibited inflammatory and scar forming signature that impeded NPC-mediated repair. Intriguingly, we unveil that NPC-derived astrocytes perform regenerative remodeling of CSPGs as they have the capacity to clear CSPGs from the ECM through receptor-mediated endocytosis (RME) and to digest CSPGs intracellularly and extracellularly by producing and releasing ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9 (A disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs-1, -9). These novel mechanisms unravel that NPC-derived astrocytes can facilitate spinal cord repair by reconditioning the injury microenvironment and suggests the benefit of astrogenesis in NPC-based therapies for SCI.

Results

Transplanted NPC-derived astrocytes exhibit a pro-regenerative phenotype associated with CSPGs degradation in SCI

We conducted parallel in vitro and in vivo experiments to determine how NPC-derived and primary spinal astrocytes behave in the milieu of SCI. We first generated human NPCs from embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and then differentiated NPCs to mature GFAP-expressing astrocytes (Figure 1A). In parallel, we maintained human primary spinal astrocytes in 10% fetal bovine serum media to become mature GFAP-expressing cells (Figure 1B). Next, we transplanted both populations of astrocytes into the spinal cord of rats with compressive SCI and studied the behavior of engrafted astrocytes after one week (Figure 1C). We identified transplanted human astrocytes in the host injured spinal cord by immunostaining for the human-specific cytoplasmic marker, STEM121 (Figure 1D). To study astrocyte pro-inflammatory phenotype, we performed immunostaining for tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), iNOS, and Nestin (Colombo and Farina, 2016; Hart and Karimi-Abdolrezaee, 2021; Hou et al., 2022). Our analysis revealed a significantly less proportion of NPC-derived astrocytes that expressed TNF-α (36.8%) (Figures 1E and 1F), IL-6 (33.6%) (Figures 1G and 1H), iNOS (18%) (Figures 1I and 1J), and Nestin (20.9%) (Figures 1K and 1L) compared to transplanted primary spinal astrocytes. We further analyzed the expression of transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) and IL-10, two major pro-regenerative markers. Our tissue analysis indicated a significantly higher percentage of engrafted NPC-derived astrocytes that expressed TGF-β (21%) (Figures 1M and 1N) and IL-10 (27.2%) (Figures 1O and 1P) as compared to primary spinal astrocytes graft.

We next assessed the capacity of these two populations of astrocytes for CSPGs production and degradation by immunohistochemical analysis of CS56 and C4S that mark CSPGs degradation by-product, respectively (Cregg et al., 2014; Fitch and Silver, 2008; Tran et al., 2022). Interestingly, the proportion of NPC-derived astrocytes that produced CSPGs (CS56) was significantly less (19.85%) (Figures 1Q–1R) than primary spinal astrocytes, while the level of CSPGs digestion (C4S) was significantly higher (2-fold) in NPC-derived astrocytes in comparison with transplanted primary spinal astrocytes (Figures 1S–1T). Collectively, our data show that NPC-derived astrocytes exhibit a pro-regenerative phenotype in the milieu of SCI and contribute to the regenerative remodeling of CSPGs, while primary spinal astrocytes are pro-inflammatory and play a major role in pathologic CSPGs remodeling.

Astrocytes derived from resident spinal cord NPCs show a distinct activation phenotype after SCI

We further studied the behavior of astrocytes generated by endogenous NPCs after SCI in a tamoxifen-inducible Nestin-CreERT2;ROSA26-mGFP transgenic mouse model that allows tracking NPC progenies in vivo. We induced dorsal hemi-section SCI in these mice to maintain the integrity of spinal cord central canal, where endogenous spinal NPCs are situated, and studied the behavior of astrocytes two weeks after SCI (Figure 2A). To avoid labeling of Nestin-expressing reactive astrocytes, we induced Cre recombination four weeks prior to SCI (Figure 2A). Tissue analysis was performed on lesion border astrocytes derived from endogenous NPCs (GFAP+/GFP+) versus spinal resident astrocytes (GFAP+/GFP−) (Figure 2A). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the majority of spinal resident astrocytes express pro-inflammatory markers including TNF-α (55.85%), IL-6 (56%), iNOS (36.1%), and Nestin (28.59%) compared to spinal NPC-derived astrocytes following SCI (Figures 2B–2I). Our tissue analysis also indicated a significantly higher percentage of spinal NPC-derived astrocytes that expressed IL-10 (25.08%) and TGF-β (33.1%) compared to spinal resident astrocytes (Figures 2J–2M). Furthermore, we found a significantly less amount of CSPGs (43.70%) and a higher level of degraded CSPGs (5.1-fold) in the areas occupied by NPC-derived astrocytes (GFAP+/GFP+) compared to the areas where only spinal resident astrocytes (GFAP+/GFP−) were present (Figures 2N and 2Q). These findings suggest that NPC-derived astrocytes show pro-regenerative phenotypes and perform regenerative CSPGs remodeling by degrading excess CSPGs in the SCI lesion compared to spinal resident astrocytes.

Transcriptomic analysis revealed a pro-regenerative phenotype for activated NPC-derived astrocytes

To substantiate our in vivo findings and elucidate cellular and molecular mechanisms, we performed global RNA sequencing on NPC-derived and primary spinal astrocytes under control and activated conditions (treated with TNF-α and IL-1β) (Hart et al., 2020) (Figure 3A). First, we found 2,597 differentially expressed genes between control NPC-derived astrocytes and control primary spinal astrocytes suggesting considerable differences between these two astrocytes populations (Figure S1A). Relative to control NPC-derived astrocytes, the majority of upregulated genes in control primary spinal astrocytes were associated with inflammatory, immune, and defense processes suggesting primary spinal astrocytes are more primed to respond to injury compared to NPC-derived astrocytes (Figure S1B). We next characterized the gene expression pattern of control versus activated NPC-derived astrocytes. Our analysis indicated 315 differentially expressed genes that are mainly involved in response to stress, external stimuli, and inflammatory responses verifying the efficacy of TNF-α/IL-1β regimen in activating NPC-derived astrocytes (Figures S1C and S1D). Notably, we found a significant increase in the expression of the ADMATS enzyme family, CSPGs degrading enzymes (Lemarchant et al., 2013), such as ADAMTS9, ADAMTS5 and, ADAMTSL3 in activated NPC-derived astrocytes compared to their control condition (Figure S1C).

Our next analysis identified 3,074 differentially expressed genes between control and activated primary spinal astrocytes (Figure S1E). Interestingly, several members of ADAMTS enzyme family were downregulated after the activation of primary spinal astrocytes (Figure S1E). Further analysis revealed that genes related to astrocytes activity such as immune and defense responses are upregulated while regenerative-associated processes including neurogenesis, neuronal projection, and synapse formation were downregulated following the activation of primary spinal astrocytes (Figure S1F).

Lastly, we detected 4,784 differentially expressed genes between activated NPC-derived and activated primary spinal astrocytes (Figure 3B). Our analysis showed upregulation of genes associated with pro-inflammatory functions of astrocytes including immune, inflammatory, and defense responses in activated primary spinal astrocytes compared with activated NPC-derived astrocytes (Figures 3C–3E). In contrast, activated NPC-derived astrocytes showed upregulation of genes related to neuroregenerative and reparative functions of astrocytes such as neurogenesis, synapse formation, and neuronal activity compared with activated primary spinal astrocytes (Figures 3C–3E). Altogether, our transcriptomics analysis also demonstrates that activated NPC-derived astrocytes show pro-regenerative phenotype, while activated primary spinal astrocytes exhibit pro-inflammatory phenotype in response to injury condition, which supports our in vivo immunohistological analysis of astrocyte phenotypes following SCI (Figures 1 and 2).

NPC-derived astrocytes exhibit a lower level of activity under inflammatory condition in vitro

We next studied the phenotype of astrocytes from both populations under TNF-α/IL-1β treatment by focusing on key markers of astrocyte reactivity (Figure S1G). Immunohistochemical quantification of Ki67, a cell proliferation marker, indicated that both NPC-derived and primary spinal astrocytes significantly increase their proliferation by responding to TNF-α/IL-1β treatment, as activation regimen (Hart and Karimi-Abdolrezaee, 2021; Hou et al., 2022), by 2.95- and 4.91-fold compared to their homeostasis condition, respectively (Figures S1H and S1I). TNF-α/IL-1β treatment also significantly enhances the expression of iNOS and Nestin, two main markers of activated astrocytes (Hart and Karimi-Abdolrezaee, 2021; Hou et al., 2022), by 4.08- and 3.58-fold increase, respectively, in primary spinal astrocytes compared with the baseline control, while the expression of Nestin and iNOS remain unchanged in NPC-derived astrocytes under TNF-α/IL-1β treatment (Figures S1J–S1M). Collectively, we show that both astrocyte populations respond to inflammatory stimuli; however, the degree of their reactivity differs.

Activated primary spinal astrocytes inhibit proliferation, growth, and metabolic activity of NPCs

To investigate how astrocytes affect the regenerative capacity of NPCs, we exposed human NPCs to conditioned media of human astrocytes in an insert-based in vitro co-culture system (Figure S2A). In this experiment, we also included a group with base media plus TNF-α/IL-1β to rule out any possible effects of TNF-α and IL-1β on NPCs. Our analysis showed that factors secreted by activated primary spinal astrocytes significantly reduce NPC growth by limiting their cell spreading and process outgrowth compared to control base media (2.30-fold), base media plus TNF-α/IL-1β (2.28-fold), and all other astrocyte conditioned media from control primary spinal astrocytes (2.15-fold) and control (2.35-fold) and activated NPC-derived astrocytes (2.18-fold) (Figures S2B and S2C). Likewise, our LIVE/DEAD assay showed that activated primary spinal astrocytes significantly inhibited NPC survival compared to control base media (34%), control base media plus TNF-α/IL-1β (36%), control primary spinal astrocytes (36%), control NPC-derived astrocytes (38%), and activated NPC astrocytes (43%) (Figures S2D and S2E).

To evaluate the proliferation capacity and metabolic activity of NPCs, EdU incorporation assay showed that activated primary spinal astrocytes significantly suppressed NPC proliferation compared to control media (34.9%), control media plus TNF-α/IL-1β (32.44%), control NPC-derived astrocytes (28.49%), activated NPC astrocytes (21.36%), and control primary spinal astrocytes (29.73%), while activated NPC-derived astrocytes did not affect the proliferation of NPCs compared to the baseline condition (Figures S2F and S2G). Likewise, assessment of protein synthesis using the O-propargyl-puromycin (OPP) assay revealed that activated primary spinal astrocytes significantly suppressed the overall metabolic activity of NPCs in -comparison with base media (40%), base media plus TNF-α/IL-1β (38.13%), and co-cultured with control NPC-derived astrocytes (49%), activated NPC-derived astrocytes (46.04%), and control primary spinal astrocytes (44.82%) (Figures S2H and S2I). Overall, these analyses showed that activated NPC-derived astrocytes have no detrimental effects on various NPC properties. These findings provide direct evidence to suggest that activated NPC-derived astrocytes can potentially promote the reparative properties of NPCs under injury condition, while activated primary spinal astrocytes inhibit NPC properties and promote their cell death.

NPC-derived astrocytes promotes neurogenesis, neuronal maturity, and synapse formation

Next, we investigated the effects of astrocytes, from both populations, on differentiation pattern of NPCs. Our analysis in astrocyte and NPC co-culture showed that activated NPC-derived astrocytes significantly enhance the neurogenesis of NPCs relative to base media (3.29-fold), base media plus TNF-α/IL-1β (4.48-fold), control NPC-derived astrocytes (5.91-fold), control primary spinal astrocytes (6.96-fold), and more robustly in comparison with activated primary spinal astrocytes (10.08-fold) (Figures S3A and S3B). We further analyzed neuronal subtypes by focusing on spinal cord-relevant neurons including motoneurons (ChAT+) and V1 (EN.1+), V2 (SCL+), V3 (SIM.1+), and dl2/4 (LHX2+) interneurons. Activated NPC-derived astrocytes significantly enhanced the differentiation of co-cultured NPCs into motoneurons (18.25%) as well as V3 (4.40%) and V2 (3.52%) spinal interneurons compared with control groups and other co-culture conditions (Figures S3C–S3H). Increase in neuronal differentiation of NPCs under the NPC-derived astrocyte milieu was accompanied by a significant decrease in NPC astrogenesis (24.04%) compared to the control group and other astrocyte populations (Figures S4A and S4B). However, we found no significant difference in V1 (Figures S3I and S3J) and dl2/4 subtypes among the control and co-culture groups (Figures S4C and S4D).

We next assessed the paracrine effects of these astrocyte populations on neuronal maturity and synapse formation of NPC-derived neurons after four weeks. For neuronal maturity, we measured the dendritic density of postsynaptic density protein-95 (PSD-95) and AMPA receptor GluA2 as two indexes of neuronal maturation (Hosseini et al., 2022; Llamosas et al., 2020). We found that control (0.30 ± 0.14 particles/100 μm2) and activated primary spinal astrocytes (0.22 ± 0.12 particles/100 μm2) significantly reduced the dendritic density of GluA2 compared with other groups (Figures S4E and S4F). In contrast, activated NPC-derived astrocytes (2.93 ± 1.08 particles/100 μm2) significantly increased the dendritic density of GluA2 by 74% compared with control NPC-derived astrocytes (1.68 ± 0.14 particles/100 μm2) (Figures S4E and S4F). Likewise, co-culture of control (0.33 ± 0.24 particles/100 μm2) and activated primary spinal astrocytes (0.28 ± 0.17 particles/100 μm2) significantly declined the dendritic density of PSD-95 of NPC-derived neurons co-cultured with astrocytes compared with other populations of astrocytes and control conditions (Figure S4G). Next, we assessed synapse formation of NPC-derived neurons in co-culture with astrocytes. Activated NPC-derived astrocytes significantly promoted the density of both excitatory synapses (50.21 ± 11.24 synapses/100 μm) and inhibitory synapses (4.52 ± 0.80 synapses/100 μm) of NPC-derived neurons compared to other conditions (Figures S4H–S4K). Collectively, our data suggest that activated NPC-derived astrocytes may facilitate repair process by promoting the differentiation of NPCs into motoneuron and V2 and V3 spinal interneurons and enhancing their maturity and synaptogenesis, which is also verified by our RNA sequencing data.

Upon activation, primary spinal astrocytes secrete excessive CSPGs, while NPC-derived astrocytes maintain a basal level of CSPG release

Given we previously reported that CSPGs inhibit NPC properties (Dyck et al., 2015, 2019; 2015; Hosseini et al., 2022), we next asked whether the level of CSPG release underlies the differential effects of primary spinal and NPC-derived astrocytes on NPCs properties. We collected astrocytes conditioned media and measured the level of laminin, as a component of the SCI matrix (Gaudet and Popovich, 2014), total CSPGs, and various CSPG subtypes using slot blot analysis (Figure 4A). Our analysis revealed that activated primary spinal astrocytes release a significantly higher level of CSPGs in their media compared with their baseline control condition (2.68-fold), and the control (2.67-fold) and activated NPC-derived astrocytes (2.94-fold) (Figure 4B). Of note, we found that activated primary spinal astrocytes produce significantly higher levels of aggrecan (6.24-fold) and versican (2.42-fold) relative to activated NPC-derived astrocytes (Figures 4C and 4D), while there was no difference in the level of neurocan, brevican, and laminin between the two populations of astrocytes (Figures S5A–S5C). We next sought to determine whether the increased secretion of CSPGs by activated primary spinal astrocytes reflects an increase in their CSPGs synthesis. Intriguingly, we did not find any significant difference in expression of genes involved in CSPGs synthesis pathway between the two types of astrocytes in RNA sequencing data (Figure 4E). This suggested that both activated primary spinal and NPC-derived astrocytes express similar levels of CSPGs. However, the lower level of CSPG release in activated NPC-derived astrocytes might be due to their ability for CSPGs digestion. This hypothesis was supported by the upregulation of CSPGs-digesting enzymes, ADAMTS, in activated NPC-derived astrocytes compared to their control (Figure S1C) and activated primary spinal astrocytes (Figure 3B). To address this question, we first exposed activated astrocytes to exogenous CSPGs to test their ability for CSPGs digestion (Figure 4F). Our immunostaining for C4S demonstrated a significantly higher level of CSPGs degradation in activated NPC-derived astrocytes compared with activated primary spinal astrocytes (2.62-fold) suggesting that activated astrocytes originated from NPCs are capable of digesting CSPGs (Figures 4G and 4H). Interestingly, this effect was significantly reduced (44.33%) when CSPGs receptors, LAR and PTP-σ, were blocked using intracellular LAR peptide (ILP) and intracellular sigma peptide (ISP) indicating the involvement of CSPG signaling in this phenomenon (Figures 4G and 4H). Taken together, our findings suggest that the contribution of activated NPC-derived astrocytes to production of pathologic CSPGs is minimal, and more importantly, these astrocytes also perform regenerative CSPGs remodeling by uptaking and digesting excess CSPGs in their injury microenvironment.

Inhibitory effects of activated primary spinal astrocytes on NPCs are mainly attributed to the secretion of CSPGs

To further substantiate the contribution of CSPGs to the paracrine inhibitory effects of activated primary spinal astrocytes on NPC properties, we blocked CSPGs production in astrocytes using xyloside (Lau et al., 2012). First, we verified that CSPGs secreted by astrocytes can pass through the co-culture insert and then identified the safety and efficacy of xyloside 100 μM in suppressing CSPGs production (Figures S5D–S5I). Interestingly, xyloside treatment reversed the inhibitory effects of activated primary spinal astrocytes on various NPC properties to the level comparable to control baseline condition. Our analysis showed a significant 2.43-fold increase in NPCs cell spreading in co-culture with activated primary spinal astrocytes under xyloside treatment compared with non-treated condition (Figures S6A and S6B). Likewise, xyloside treatment significantly enhanced the survival (37.80%), proliferation (23.28%), and protein synthesis (2.5-fold) of NPCs co-cultured with activated primary spinal astrocytes (Figures S6C–S6H). These findings verified that activated primary spinal astrocytes negatively regulate the properties and activity of NPCs by secreting excess amounts of CSPGs in their conditioned media. In conclusion, our complementary data provide strong evidence that activated primary spinal astrocytes inhibit the regenerative capacities of NPCs through excess production and secretion of CSPGs.

Activated NPC-derived astrocytes uptake CSPGs through RME and digest CSPGs

We next sought to determine if activated NPC-derived astrocytes can uptake, digest, and remove excess CSPGs from the ECM. We exposed astrocytes to exogenous CSPGs conjugated to Alexa Fluor 568 to track CSPG endocytosis (Figures S7A and S7B). After 24 h of activation, the proportion of activated NPC-derived astrocytes that performed CSPGs endocytosis and the amount of internalized CSPGs were significantly higher than activated primary spinal astrocytes (33.69% and 9.85-fold, respectively) (Figures S7C–S7E). Interestingly, blockade of the CSPG/LAR/PTP-σ axis by ILP and ISP treatment markedly reduced the percentage of activated NPC-derived astrocytes performing endocytosis by 23% and the amount of internalized CSPGs per each astrocyte by 73.6% (Figures S7C–S7E). These data identified RME as a key underlying mechanism for the uptake of CSPGs by activated NPC-derived astrocytes. Notably, our immunostaining also showed co-localization of C4S with fluorescently labeled CSPGs inside activated NPC-derived astrocytes, verifying that they had digested the endocytosed CSPGs and highlighting the capacity of these astrocytes in the uptake and degradation of CSPGs from the extracellular environment (Figure S7F).

Our transcriptomics analysis also revealed upregulation of RME-related genes such as EEA1, TFAP2A, SH3GL3, KIF5A, and KIF5C in activated NPC-derived astrocytes relative to activated primary spinal astrocytes (Figure S7G). Although we show that LAR and PTP-σ receptors are involved in CSPGs endocytosis by activated NPC-derived astrocytes, we did not find any difference in the expression levels of CSPGs receptors between the two populations suggesting differences in their functions in activated NPC-derived astrocytes versus activated primary spinal astrocytes (Figures S7H and S7I). To determine whether a difference in the general endocytosis capacity of astrocytes might affect RME of CSPGs, we treated both astrocyte populations with plastic beads. Our analysis showed that all astrocytes across various conditions were able to perform bead endocytosis in a comparable manner (Figures S7J and S7K), ruling out that the general endocytosis capacity of astrocytes might underlie CSPGs endocytosis. To further validate the specificity of RME as a mechanism for CSPG endocytosis by activated NPC-derived astrocytes, we treated these astrocytes with the inhibitor of AP-2 complex, Barbadin, which blocks the REM process (Beautrait et al., 2017). We found that Barbadin treatment significantly reduces (5.95-fold) the internalization of CSPGs by activated NPC-derived astrocytes (Figures 4I and 4J). These findings identified RME as an underlying mechanism for CSPG endocytosis by activated NPC-derived astrocytes that is mediated by LAR and PTP-σ receptors (Figures 4I and 4J).

Activated NPC-derived astrocytes facilitate regenerative CSPGs remodeling through the production and release of ADAMTS1/9

Since our RNA sequencing dataset showed upregulation of ADAMTS family in activated NPC-derived astrocytes (Figure 3B), we next asked whether these astrocytes can also perform CSPGs remodeling extracellularly in their milieu through paracrine secretion of ADAMTS enzymes. To address this question, we cultured and activated astrocytes and transferred their conditioned media to culture wells coated with CSPGs labeled with Alexa Fluor 568 without any cells (Figure 5A). Our immunostaining revealed CSPGs degradation only in wells treated with conditioned media of activated NPC-derived astrocytes (Figure 5B).

To unravel the role of ADAMTS enzymes in CSPGs degradation by activated NPC-derived astrocytes, we first studied the expression levels of different subtypes of ADAMTS family using quantitative real-time PCR (Figure 5C). We found that ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9 are significantly upregulated in activated NPC-derived astrocytes compared to their control condition (30.48%) and activated primary spinal astrocytes (88.31%) (Figure 5D). Our further protein analysis showed that activated NPC-derived astrocytes spontaneously (i.e., without exposure to exogenous CSPGs) release a significantly higher level of ADAMTS1 compared with control (3.12-fold) and activated primary spinal astrocytes (4.09-fold) (Figure 5E). Blocking LAR and PTP-σ receptors had no effect on the secretion of ADAMTS1 when astrocytes were not exposed to exogenous CSPGs (Figure 5E). Interestingly, adding exogenous CSPGs to astrocyte culture enhanced the release of ADAMTS1 by activated NPC-derived astrocytes (1.91-fold), suggesting that these astrocytes are responsive to increased levels of CSPGs in the microenvironment (Figure 5E). Under CSPGs exposure, the secretion of ADAMTS1 by activated NPC-derived astrocytes was significantly suppressed (50.39%) by inhibiting LAR and PTP-σ receptors indicating a role for CSPG/LAR/PTP-σ axis in this process (Figure 5E). We found that activated NPC-derived astrocytes (without CSPGs treatment) secrete a significantly higher level of ADAMTS9 in comparison with their control baseline condition (2-fold), which was slightly but not significantly reduced by suppressing LAR and PTP-σ receptors (Figure 5F). We also found that exogenous CSPGs slightly (but not significantly) increased the secretion of ADAMTS9 by activated NPC-derived astrocytes, which was significantly inhibited (68.37%) by ILP/ISP treatment (Figure 5F).

To further validate the role of ADAMTS1/9 in CSPGs remodeling by activated NPC-derived astrocytes, we used dicer small interfering RNA (siRNA) to downregulate ADAMTS1/9 in astrocytes (Figure 6A). First, we confirmed the efficacy of astrocyte transfection by using fluorescently labeled siRNAs and identified the most efficient siRNA, siADAMTS9-3, and siADAMTS1-2, among several candidates (Figures S7L–S7N). We also used a non-targeted siRNA control to account for any effects of siRNA transfection per se. Downregulation of ADAMTS9 significantly increased the secretion of CSPGs (2.38-fold) by activated NPC-derived astrocytes compared to non-targeted siRNA (Figure 6B). Downregulation of ADAMTS1 also partly but not significantly enhanced CSPGs secretion of activated NPC-derived astrocytes (Figure 6B). We also evaluated CSPGs degradation in these experiments by slot blot analysis of C4S in astrocyte cell lysate and conditioned media. Without exposure to exogenous CSPGs, we found a significantly higher level of CSPGs by-products in the conditioned media of activated NPC-derived astrocytes (0.24 ± 0.02) and their cell lysate (0.28 ± 0.09) compared with other astrocyte populations in control and non-targeted siRNA groups (Figures 6C and 6D). Interestingly, downregulation of ADAMTS9 significantly suppressed the intracellular digestion of CSPGs in activated NPC-derived astrocytes compared with non-treated (66.53%) and non-targeted siRNA (59.07%) when astrocytes were not exposed to exogenous CSPGs (Figure 6D). Downregulation of ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9 partly (but not significantly) decreased C4S expression in the conditioned media of activated NPC-derived astrocytes (Figure 6C).

We further determined whether the paracrine secretion of ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9 by activated NPC-derived astrocytes can promote the digestion of extracellular CSPGs. After exposing to CSPGs, activated NPC-derived astrocytes in control non-siRNA-treated condition digested a significantly higher amount of CSPGs in their conditioned media compared to their control baseline state (1.37-fold), control primary spinal astrocytes (2.12-fold), and activated primary spinal astrocytes (2.17-fold) (Figure 6E). Downregulation of ADAMTS1 or ADAMTS9 significantly inhibited the extracellular digestion of CSPGs in the conditioned media of activated NPC-derived astrocytes by 57.34% and 61.26%, respectively, compared to non-targeted siRNA condition (Figure 6E). This finding highlights that the capacity of NPC-derived astrocytes for CSPGs remodeling of ECM is mediated by the paracrine release of ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9.

Since we showed that activated NPC-derived astrocytes also digest CSPGs intracellularly after RME, we sought to determine whether ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9 also mediate this process. Our analysis on the cell lysate of activated NPC-derived astrocytes showed a significant increase in digestion of exogenous CSPGs compared to control (2.41-fold) and activated primary spinal astrocyte (3.44-fold) in the control non-siRNA-treated group (Figure 6F). Similarly, digestion of CSPGs was significantly higher in cell lysate of activated NPC-derived astrocytes compared with control (1.67-fold) and activated primary spinal astrocytes (1.75-fold) in the non-targeted siRNA group (Figure 6F). Of note, siRNA downregulation of ADAMTS1 or ADAMTS9 significantly blocked the intracellular digestion of exogenous CSPGs by 50.25% and 54.85%, respectively, in activated NPC-derived astrocytes in comparison with the non-targeted siRNA group (Figure 6F). This observation confirms that activated NPC-derived astrocytes can also intracellularly digest the exogenous CSPGs that they internalized by RME by the production of ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9. Altogether, our extensive in vitro findings show that while both NPC-derived and primary spinal astrocytes comparably express CSPGs after activation, the capacity of NPC-derived astrocytes to increase their production and release of ADAMTS1/9 leads to lower CSPG deposition in ECM through intracellular and extracellular degradation of CSPGs.

To validate the contribution of ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9 in regenerative CSPGs remodeling by NPC-derived astrocytes after SCI, we performed immunohistochemical analysis on transplanted injured spinal cord tissue. Interestingly, our tissue analysis revealed a significantly higher level of ADAMTS1 (3.34-fold) and ADAMTS9 (3.10-fold) in engrafted NPC-derived astrocytes compared to engrafted primary spinal astrocytes (Figures 6G–6J). Our tissue assessments indicated that 28.57% of ADAMTS1 was co-localized with NPC-derived astrocytes and 71.42% was released extracellularly by NPC-derived astrocytes (Figures 7A–7C). Similarly, we detected 35.96% of ADAMTS9 co-localized with NPC-derived astrocytes intracellularly and 64.03% was released extracellularly (Figures 7D–7F). We only detected negligible signal for ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9 in engrafted primary spinal astrocytes group (Figures 7A, 7B, 7D, and 7E). To verify the efficacy of ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9 enzymes in CSPG degradation, we evaluated the co-localization of ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9 with C4S. We found that 89.46% of ADAMTS1 (Figures 7G and 7H) and 89.98% of ADAMTS9 (Figures 7I and 7J) were co-localized with C4S, suggesting the role of these enzymes in CSPGs remodeling in the injured spinal cord.

In conclusion, our in vitro and in vivo findings collectively suggest the capacity of activated NPC-derived astrocytes to digest CSPGs that they produce after activation. Expression of ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9 seems to be an underlying mechanism for lower secretion of CSPGs by these astrocytes in the injury microenvironment. Moreover, our findings indicate that activated NPC-derived astrocytes perform regenerative CSPGs remodeling in their microenvironment by releasing ADAMTS1/ADAMTS9 in the ECM, which can further degrade inhibitory CSPGs in the injured spinal cord.

Discussion

Extensive evidence shows that the injured spinal cord milieu inherently favors astrogenesis of resident or transplanted NPCs at the expense of oligogenesis and neurogenesis (Hosseini et al., 2022; Karimi-Abdolrezaee et al., 2006, 2010; Khazaei et al., 2020). This has been viewed undesirable as pre-existing resident astrocytes increase their number after SCI by astrogliosis, and generation of additional astrocytes seems unnecessary in the injured spinal cord where oligodendrocytes and neurons are lost (Ahuja et al., 2020; Alizadeh et al., 2019; Assinck et al., 2017; Hosseini et al., 2024; Karimi-Abdolrezaee and Billakanti, 2012). It is also thought that overproduction of astrocytes might be potentially detrimental as activated astrocytes can promote injury and inflammation directly, or indirectly by affecting other cell types such as microglia, and limit post-injury repair processes by altering the ECM and scar formation (Gaudet and Fonken, 2018; Hart and Karimi-Abdolrezaee, 2021; Wang et al., 2022). Importantly, activated astrocytes are a main contributor to the long-lasting over-production of inhibitory CSPGs following injury (Gaudet and Fonken, 2018; Hart and Karimi-Abdolrezaee, 2021). In this study, we have addressed an existing knowledge gap on how NPC-derived astrocytes behave in the injury milieu and deconstructed their contributions to the secondary injury mechanisms after SCI. We provide novel evidence that (1) astrocytes originated from endogenous or transplanted NPCs show an immune modulatory and pro-repair inflammatory profile under injury condition that is distinct from the pro-inflammatory signature of resident primary spinal astrocytes, (2) NPC-derived astrocytes perform regenerative remodeling of ECM as they can endocytose and degrade inhibitory CSPGs through LAR and PTP-σ receptors, and production and release of ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9, a capacity that resident spinal astrocytes lack, and (3) unlike activated primary spinal astrocytes that impede neurogenesis and synaptogenesis, upon activation, NPC-derived astrocytes provide a supportive milieu that fosters NPC-mediated repair in SCI. These mechanisms are particularly important in protecting and repairing the injured spinal cord.

Astrocytes are known to play a major role in the onset of neuroinflammation after SCI (Burda et al., 2022). Within the first hours, resident astrocytes respond to the injury by communicating with microglia, and together they produce a host of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Pineau and Lacroix, 2007), which disrupts the blood-spinal barrier and facilitates leukocyte infiltration into the SCI lesion (Hellenbrand et al., 2021; Lawrence et al., 1998; Pineau and Lacroix, 2007; Sofroniew, 2020). Single-cell RNA sequencing has shown that most of spinal astrocytes overexpress neuroinflammatory and scar-forming mediators such as complement3 (C3), NF-κβ, and STAT3 (signal transducer and activator of transcription 3) following SCI (Hou et al., 2022; Leng et al., 2022). Consistent with this evidence, our in vivo data show that spinal resident astrocytes exhibit injurious phenotypes by producing TNF-α, IL-6, and iNOS. Of note, increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines promote cell death and neurodegeneration (Ferguson et al., 2008; Lawrence et al., 1998). In contrast, we demonstrate that NPC-derived astrocytes present an inflammation-resolving phenotype in SCI due to higher expression of immune regulatory mediators, IL-10 and TGF-β. Consistent with our findings, O’Shea and colleagues reported that astrocytes originated from transplanted NPCs reduce scarring and enhance bridge formation in a mouse model of hemi-section SCI (O’Shea et al., 2022). Likewise, NPC-derived astrocytes can reduce C3 and C5b9 complexes following brain injury (Gao et al., 2018). Thus, NPC-mediated astrogenesis appears to aid in resolving neuroinflammation and providing a permissive environment for tissue repair.

Astrocytes are a key contributor to the production and deposition of pathologic CSPGs following SCI (Tran et al., 2018). CSPGs deposits are long-standing in spinal cord lesions, which impede endogenous repair processes in SCI and also restrict the regenerative capacity of engrafted NPCs in the injured spinal cord (Barritt et al., 2006; Bradbury et al., 2002; Dyck et al., 2015, 2019; Hosseini et al., 2022; Karimi-Abdolrezaee et al., 2010; Lang et al., 2015). Here, our in vitro studies verify that activated primary spinal astrocytes significantly inhibit NPCs growth, metabolism, and differentiation through paracrine secretion of CSPGs. In contrast, NPC-derived astrocytes do not exert any inhibitory effects on the regenerative properties of NPCs due to a significantly lower level of CSPGs release by these astrocytes. These findings provide novel evidence that NPC-mediated astrogenesis fosters a permissive microenvironment for the survival and integration of endogenous and engrafted NPCs in the host injured spinal cord.

A key finding of this study is unraveling the capacity of NPC-derived astrocytes for clearance and degradation of inhibitory CSPGs. In addition to their lower deposition of CSPGs, we show that NPC-derived astrocytes can respond to the exogenous CSPGs in their vicinity and have the capability to uptake the excess CSPGs from the ECM through RME and digestion of internalized CSPGs by ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9 enzymes. This ability of activated NPC-derived astrocytes seems to underlie the lower release of CSPGs that we detected in the conditioned media of these astrocytes. Interestingly, the inability of primary spinal astrocytes to endocytose CSPGs was not due to the lack of LAR and PTP-σ; however, these receptors appear to function differently in these astrocytes, which needs further elucidation. Importantly, we provide novel evidence that in response to excess CSPGs in their extracellular microenvironment, activated NPC-derived astrocytes release ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9, as two CSPG-degrading enzymes (Lemarchant et al., 2013), and extracellularly digest CSPGs in the ECM. This rise in ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9 in activated NPC-derived astrocytes might reflect the higher level of TGF-β expression since TGF-β is shown to regulate the expression of several ADAMTS enzymes in other cell types including osteosarcoma, prostate cells, and tendon-derived cells (Corps et al., 2008; Cross et al., 2005; Rose et al., 2021; Surridge et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2003). Intriguingly, suppression of LAR and PTP-σ receptors in NPC-derived astrocytes significantly diminished the production and release of ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9 suggesting a plausible role of the CSPG/LAR/PTP-σ axis in this mechanism. In other studies, overexpression of ADAMTS4 in resident astrocytes using AAV-5 significantly decreased the lesion size and enhanced the regeneration of host corticospinal tract and serotonergic fibers in a rat model of contusion SCI (Griffin et al., 2020). Likewise, the administration of ADAMTS4 significantly enhances axonal regeneration and sprouting and improves functional recovery in rat SCI (Lemarchant et al., 2014; Tauchi et al., 2012). Here, our new findings suggest the capacity of endogenous or transplanted NPC-derived astrocytes in remodeling of the pathologic CSPGs in the lesion microenvironment through the production of ADAMTS with the potential to promote repair mechanisms after SCI.

We demonstrate that NPC-derived astrocytes have a major role in regulating neurogenesis of NPCs, in particular motoneurons and V2 and V3 spinal interneurons, and they also promote maturation and synapse formation of new neurons. In the developing and adult CNS, astrocytes are a major cell type in NPC niches, which modulate and support neurogenesis (Araki et al., 2021; Olude et al., 2015). Under physiological condition, astrocytes regulate neuronal activities, synaptic transmission, and functions of neural circuits by forming a tripartite synapse, uptaking neurotransmitters, and secreting various growth factors and cytokines (Chen et al., 2023; Durkee et al., 2021; Khakh and Sofroniew, 2015). Consistent with our data, it is shown that astrocytes derived from human NPCs are crucial for maturation of excitatory synapse, excitability, and synchronous network activity of human NPC-derived neurons (Klapper et al., 2019). Notably, single-cell RNA sequencing has confirmed overexpression of neurotrophic factors and genes related to synapse formation in astrocytes derived from spinal NPCs in mouse SCI (Wei et al., 2023). Implantation of neonatal astrocytes diminishes the glial scar and stimulates the repair process after SCI (Joosten et al., 2004; Smith and Silver, 1988; Smith et al., 1987). The substrate produced by neonatal astrocytes is permissive to neurons and facilitates the neurite outgrowth of neurons in vitro (McKeon et al., 1991; Rudge and Silver, 1990). Neonatal astrocytes also form bridges between two borders of the spinal lesion facilitating axonal regeneration after SCI (Haas et al., 2012; Smith and Silver, 1988). On the other hand, activated resident astrocytes in the forebrain lose their physiological and supportive functions that reduce the survival of neurons and inhibit excitatory synapse formation (Liddelow et al., 2017). Capitalizing on the new insights discovered in our study, we propose that NPC-derived astrocytes present a profile that resembles the characteristics of supportive astrocytes during CNS development and homeostasis.

In conclusion, we provide novel insights into the mechanisms by which NPC-derived astrocytes promote SCI repair. Astrocytes originated from NPCs perform regenerative remodeling of CSPGs at sites of SCI lesions by internalizing CSPGs through LAR and PTP-σ-mediated RME, and producing and releasing CSPG-degrading enzymes, ADAMTS1 and ADAMTS9. Paracrine secretion of NPC-derived astrocytes is permissive to NPC growth and survival and enhances differentiation of NPCs into spinal cord-specific neurons and fosters neuronal maturity and synaptogenesis of new neurons. Our new findings signify the importance of astrogenesis by endogenous and engrafted NPCs for neuroregeneration after SCI. We also show the significance of promoting the multilineage capacity of NPCs to support both neurogenesis and gliogenesis in SCI. Future work is needed to dissect the role of NPC-derived astrocytes in the functional integration of NPC-derived neurons into the host spinal circuitry and axon regeneration and to investigate their contribution to re-the establishment of the astrocyte-neuron network after SCI.

Experimental procedures

Animals

Animal experiments were performed in compliance with policies and approved protocols by the Animal Care Committee of the University of Manitoba according to the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care (Protocol # 22-017). For in vivo studies, in total six adult female Sprague-Dawley rats (250–300 g) and four transgenic Nestin-CreERT2; ROSA26-mGFP mice (8-week-old) were used. Animals were housed in standard plastic cages with free access to food and drinking water in a 12/12 light-dark cycle.

Differentiation of H9 ESCs to human NPC

Human H9 ESCs (catalog # WA09, WiCell, USA) at passage 40–45 were differentiated toward caudalized spinal NPCs by dual-SMAD inhibition approach using 3 μM SB43542 (catalog #S4317, MilliporeSigma), 2 μM LDN193189 (catalog # SML0559, MilliporeSigma), 3 μM CHIR (catalog # SML1046, MilliporeSigma), and 2 μM retinoic acid (catalog# R2625, MilliporeSigma (Chambers et al., 2009; Nemati et al., 2011; Tomishima, 2008). The details of the different components of media can be found in supplemental information.

Library preparation and RNA sequencing were performed using NovaSeq 600 PE 100 (Illumina). In vitro assessment of CSPGs endocytosis by astrocytes

CSPGs (catalog # CC117, Sigma-Aldrich) were conjugated with Alexa Fluor 568 using Conjugation Kit-Lightning-Link (catalog # ab269821, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Fluorescent latex beads (catalog #L9902, Sigma-Aldrich) were used as a control for CSPG endocytosis. LAR and PTP-σ receptors were blocked by 2 μM ILP and 2 μM ISP, respectively (Dyck et al., 2018; Hosseini et al., 2022; Lang et al., 2015). For functional verification, Barbadin (20 μM, catalog # SML3127, Sigma-Aldrich) was used to block RME before astrocyte activation and during the experiment. 8–10 randomly taken images (under 20X) were used for cellular quantification using ImageJ software.

Indirect co-culture of astrocytes and NPCs

To co-culture astrocytes and NPCs, NPCs were plated at 15,000 cells/cm2 on 24-well plate coated with PLL and laminin as described earlier. Astrocytes were also seeded at 15,000 cells/cm2 onto the polycarbonate Transwell culture insert (catalog # 3412, Corning, NY, USA) coated with PLL (Renaud and Martinoli, 2016; Shahriary et al., 2019). Both populations were maintained in serum-free astrocytes media.

For quantification, the integrated density of OPP signal was calculated with ImageJ in 8–10 random images (under 20X) and was normalized to the total number of DAPI+ cells (Carvajal Ibañez et al., 2023).

Clip compression SCI in rats

To induce SCI, we used a well-established clinically relevant clip compression model in rats (Alizadeh et al., 2017; Dyck et al., 2019; Hart et al., 2020; Hosseini et al., 2022; Karimi-Abdolrezaee et al., 2006, 2010). As previously described, animals’ spinal cords were compressed at the level of T7 for 1 min using a 35 g aneurysm clip (University Health Network, Toronto, Ontario, Canada). The details of the surgical procedure and pain management are described in the supplemental information.

Transplantation of human astrocytes in rats with SCI

Transplantation of astrocytes was conducted 10–14 days after SCI (Hosseini et al., 2022; Karimi-Abdolrezaee et al., 2006; 2010). As described before, each animal received 4 injections of astrocytes (2.5 × 105 astrocytes per injection) on the rostral and caudal side of the injury site (2 mm away from injury site, each side 2 bilateral injections) using a Hamilton syringe connected to a glass micropipette. To prevent rejection of engrafted human astrocytes, SCI rats received daily subcutaneous injection of tacrolimus (3 mg/kg, catalog# B2143, APEx Bio).

Hemi-section SCI surgery in Nestin-CreERT2; ROSA26-mGFP (mT/mG) mice

Mice received daily tamoxifen injection (180 mg/kg, catalog # 5648, MilliporeSigma) for 5 days followed by 4 weeks of wash out period before SCI surgery (Figure 2A). We performed dorsal hemi-section SCI in mice at the level of T7-8 while preserving ependyma and subependymal of the central canal. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in an unbiased manner by examiners blinded to experimental conditions using Prism 8.0. To compare the data between two groups, Student’s t test was used.
